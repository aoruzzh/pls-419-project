Who is affected, and how it is changing

  • Families near the B threshold. The score adds points in steps, so a small rise in income can move a family across the line (Section 2, Figure 1).
  • Families the databases cannot see clearly. UNDP, using Ministry data from 2022, reported that more than 980,000 of 5.9 million families had no official income at all (UNDP Kazakhstan, n.d.). Informal earnings are invisible to a registry.
  • Families with outdated records. A car sold but still registered, or a relative who moved away, can change a score. In October 2026 the Minister approved new rules on the completeness and accuracy of DFC data, which shows the government sees this risk too (Zakon.kz, 2026c).
  • Everyone, because the rule keeps moving. The methodology was approved in 2023, amended in June 2026, and revised again after the Minister's announcement on 29 October 2026 (Informburo.kz, 2026b).

The social-science question

When state registries define who counts as being in need, who bears the cost of missing or outdated records, and who has the power to inspect, correct or appeal a score? This matters especially for families living on informal income, families with stale registry records and households near the A/B exclusion threshold.

James Scott (1998) argued that modern states govern what they can make "legible": what they can count, map and record. The DFC is a striking case. It lets the state see millions of families at once, but only through the records it holds. The question inside this policy is therefore about power and institutions as much as money: when eligibility is decided by what state databases can see, who becomes invisible or misread, and can citizens contest a number they are never shown?

Kazakhstan's own Law on Artificial Intelligence, in force since January 2026, gives people the right to be told when decisions about them are made with AI and to object (Legal500, 2025; Astana Times, 2025). Whether a points-based scoring rule counts as such a system is unsettled, which is part of the problem.

02 / Background & data

What the state measures, and what the numbers show

The DFC applies the Methodology for Determining the Social Well-being of a Family (Person), Ministry of Labour Order No. 267 of 29 June 2023, developed with UNDP (Zakon.uchet.kz, 2023; Ministry of Labour, 2023). UNDP and ITU describe it as based on the Alkire–Foster multidimensional poverty method (ITU, n.d.). Press reports describe 59 indicators across eight areas: income and loans, transport, employment, social protection, health, education, housing and agriculture (Azattyq Rýhy, 2026b; Informburo.kz, 2026a). Points are added or subtracted for each, and the total sets the category.

Where the data is silent

The rule itself
Sources disagree on basic facts: 30 or more than 120 source databases (Kapital.kz, 2026; ITU, 2025); 59, 80 or more than 100 indicators (Azattyq Rýhy, 2026b; UNDP Kazakhstan, n.d.; ITU, n.d.); 10, 14 or 39 benefits; five or seven categories. A citizen cannot easily find the full, current points table.
Informal income
Families paid in cash, or working abroad, look poorer or richer than they are depending on what the registries record.
Data errors
No public figures exist on how many DFC records are wrong, how many families have asked for a correction, or how many appeals succeeded.
Error rates
The government has published the savings it expects, but not an estimate of how many needy families might be scored A or B. That figure needs survey microdata (the Household Budget Survey) linked to DFC categories, which is not public.
Bank data
From 2027, banks will pass account data on benefit applicants to the state with their consent (Sputnik Kazakhstan, 2026). How that data will be weighted is not published.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

Price and MRP data are official and reliable. The scoring rule is reliable in outline (five categories, the A/B cut-off) but uncertain in detail, because the points circulating in the press predate the October 2026 revision. Savings figures are political estimates, not costed projections. Error rates are unknown. That is the gap this project addresses.

03 / Current state of research

Four ways of studying who gets help

Research on deciding who receives social assistance falls into four approaches. Each answers a different question, and each leaves something the others must fill.

1. The economics of targeting: every method makes mistakes

Economists have long compared ways of targeting the poor. Hanna and Olken (2018) show that means tests and proxy means tests (PMTs) exclude many poor households, and that the gains from targeting must be weighed against those errors and against simpler universal transfers. Testing PMTs in nine African countries, Brown, Ravallion and van de Walle (2018) find they filter out the non-poor well but miss many of the poor, especially the poorest; a simple demographic scorecard often does as well. In Indonesia, Alatas et al. (2012) found community-based targeting slightly less accurate than a PMT by consumption measures, but more satisfying to local residents. McBride and Nichols (2018) show that PMTs built to predict well out of sample, using cross-validation and ensemble methods, perform substantially better. Grosh et al. (2022) summarise the lesson for governments: no single method is best, and every choice trades accuracy against cost, speed and legitimacy.

What this means for Kazakhstan: the DFC is a kind of PMT built on administrative data. The literature predicts it will make both kinds of error, and that the question is how many and for whom.

2. Machine learning and new data: better than crude rules, worse than good registries

A newer literature uses machine learning (ML) on non-traditional data. In Togo's Novissi programme, which paid COVID-19 relief, ML on mobile-phone and satellite data reduced exclusion errors by 4–21% compared with the geographic targeting options the government considered. It would have increased them by 9–35% compared with a full social registry, which Togo lacked (Aiken et al., 2022). In Afghanistan, ML on phone records identified ultra-poor households nearly as accurately as survey-based consumption measures (Aiken et al., 2023). Comparing Bangladesh and Togo, Aiken et al. (2025) find PMTs most accurate, with algorithms more cost-effective only where many households must be screened on a small budget. Working with governments in Latin America, Noriega-Campero et al. (2020) show ML can improve both accuracy and fairness, but institutions disagreed on what "fair" should mean.

What this means for Kazakhstan: Kazakhstan already has something close to a full registry. The evidence suggests ML should not replace the DFC but could check it, for example by measuring its errors against survey data.

3. Access and administrative burden: eligible people still miss out

Being eligible is not the same as receiving help. Ko and Moffitt (2022) review take-up worldwide and find that information gaps, stigma and administrative barriers leave many eligible people without benefits. Herd and Moynihan (2018) call these learning, compliance and psychological costs "administrative burden", and show they fall hardest on those with the least time and resources. New work tests whether AI can lower these costs. In a randomised experiment with Los Angeles caseworkers, accurate chatbot suggestions raised the accuracy of answers on benefit eligibility by 27 percentage points, but wrong suggestions sharply reduced accuracy on questions caseworkers would otherwise have got right (Gosciak et al., 2026).

4. Critical and sociotechnical research: who controls the score

A fourth approach asks what scoring does to citizens and the state. Eubanks (2018) documents how automated eligibility systems in the United States shifted risk onto poor families. Selbst et al. (2019) warn that fairness cannot be engineered into a model in isolation from the institution that uses it. Scott (1998) explains why states simplify people into categories they can administer. Legal cases show the stakes: a Dutch court halted the SyRI welfare-risk system in 2020 for violating the right to private life (Hague District Court, 2020), and Australia's Royal Commission found the Robodebt scheme's automated debts inaccurate and unlawful (Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme, 2023).

Kazakhstan and the region

Evidence on Kazakhstan comes mostly from evaluations by international organisations: UNICEF's evaluation of child and family social protection for 2015–2020, which reports that the number of children among social assistance recipients fell sharply after 2018 reforms (UNICEF Kazakhstan, n.d.), and the World Bank's 2024 Poverty and Equity Assessment, prepared with the government's planning agency (World Bank, 2024a). The DFC has won prizes from the World Government Summit and ITU (UNDP Kazakhstan, 2024), but prizes are not evaluations. I found no independent, peer-reviewed study of how accurately the DFC scores families.

In Kazakhstan, an econometric study found that existing targeted assistance had a limited effect on living standards and identified low awareness, especially in rural areas (Daulieva and Sagynbay, 2024). Interviews with caseworkers and observations at service centres identified documentation problems, complex eligibility rules, and limited staff resources (Gutova et al., 2026). In neighbouring Uzbekistan, a digital registry connected eligibility checks to other state databases, yet applicants still faced overlapping procedures across programmes (World Bank, 2024b). These studies connect automation to administrative burden. The rate of misclassification by Kazakhstan’s Digital Family Card remains an open question.

Known

all targeting excludes some of the poor; registries beat ML where they exist; access costs keep eligible people out.

Contested

whether targeting is worth its errors compared with universal benefits, and what fairness means.

Gap

Kazakhstan now uses a nationwide scoring system to remove benefits, yet nobody outside government has measured how often it is wrong, for whom, or whether families can understand and contest it. This project designs the tools to do that.

04 / Solution & costs

The solution: audit the score, fix its data, explain it to families

The proposal does not replace the DFC or let AI decide who receives a benefit. It adds three AI tools around the existing rule. Each targets a different failure: nobody knows how often the score is wrong; wrong records lower scores; families cannot see or contest their score.

How the proposed tools would work

Design rule: AI is never on the path that removes a benefit. A data-quality flag can only trigger a review that may restore support. This avoids the pattern of the Dutch SyRI system, where data-linking was used to hunt for fraud.

The explainer could run on ISSAI KAZ-LLM, the open-source model released by Nazarbayev University's Institute of Smart Systems and AI in December 2024 for Kazakh, Russian and English (ISSAI, 2024). A local model keeps citizens' data inside Kazakhstan.

Real cases this builds on

Togo · Novissi

Deployed nationally to pay COVID-19 relief; independently evaluated in Nature. ML cut exclusion errors by 4–21% against geographic targeting, but did worse than a full registry would have (Aiken et al., 2022). Lesson for Kazakhstan: use ML to audit a registry, not to replace it.

United Kingdom · GOV.UK Chat

In two public pilots over 18 months, more than 10,000 users asked about 26,000 questions on services including benefits. Measured accuracy rose from 76% to 90%; the tool answers only from official guidance, shows source links and warnings, and users asked for a route to a human advisor (Government Digital Service, 2026). Lesson: an explainer must cite its source and hand over to people. Note that these figures are reported by the government team itself.

Los Angeles

A randomised experiment shows accurate chatbots help caseworkers, while wrong ones do real harm (Gosciak et al., 2026).

SyRI and Robodebt

SyRI (Hague District Court, 2020) and Robodebt, which issued around 470,000 wrongful debts before it ended in 2020 (Jurist, 2023; Royal Commission, 2023), show what happens when automated welfare decisions lack transparency and human review.

The bill

Environmental

The audit runs a few times a year. The largest compute load would come from answering questions after families receive their categories. During a pilot, meter electricity per thousand answers and ask the data centre for cooling-water use. Compare the 8-billion-parameter KAZ-LLM, a larger model, and a plain-language rules page. Use Kazakhstan’s grid mix to estimate emissions. The coal share and actual operating figures still need verification.

Risk level · Set by author

Economic

Costs are building the tools, hosting, data-sharing agreements, the independent audit team, caseworker time to review flags, and maintenance every time the rule changes. A cost estimate still needs a ministry staffing and hosting budget. The ministry would pay for implementation and review. A vendor could earn a recurring contract and gain control over maintenance. The DFC is run by the state company National Information Technologies with UNDP (ITU, n.d.). Compare the pilot with publishing the full points table, staffing a review desk and publishing appeals statistics.

Risk level · Set by author

Labor

Caseworkers gain new work: reviewing flags and handling escalations from the explainer. That is a cost, but also the human check the system needs. Hidden labor includes cleaning registry data, translating and testing Kazakh answers, and labelling records to train the flag models. Front-line staff risk deskilling if they defer to the machine; the Los Angeles study shows people follow wrong suggestions too (Gosciak et al., 2026).

Risk level · Set by author

Governance

Kazakhstan's Law No. 230-VIII "On Artificial Intelligence", signed on 17 November 2025 and in force since 18 January 2026, sorts AI systems by risk and gives people the right to know about automated processing, to object to it, and to an explanation (EY Kazakhstan, 2025; Legal500, 2025). An AI tool touching welfare eligibility would likely count as high-risk, which points to independent audit. The bigger risk is surveillance creep: adding bank data and AI flags to a database on 20 million people builds capacity that could be used for control rather than support. Control must sit with an independent auditor, with published results.

Risk level · Set by author

Data ethics

The audit needs survey data linked to DFC categories, which requires a legal basis and strict anonymisation. The flags and the explainer use data the state already holds, but families must be told and able to correct it. Proxy discrimination is a real risk: indicators such as region, housing type or family size can stand in for ethnicity or rural poverty. Every model must report error rates by group.

05 / Verdict

Only under conditions

Independent audit

Yes

Run independently of the Ministry; results published yearly, including uncertainty.

Data-quality flags

Yes, with review

Flags may only restore support; a caseworker confirms each; families are notified.

Citizen explainer

Pilot only

Scale up only if a test shows high accuracy in Kazakh and more successful corrections or appeals.

Kazakhstan should not use AI to decide who is needy; it already has a rule for that. It should use AI to check whether the rule is right and to help people challenge it when it is not. If the government will not publish the points table or allow an independent audit, the honest answer is no: AI would only make an opaque system faster.
